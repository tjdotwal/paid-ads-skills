# Format detail — templates 43–53

Full specs for the formats that carry more than a line of methodology. Templates 1–42 are self-explanatory from the one-line descriptors in `SKILL.md`.

Each entry covers what the format is, its structure, and the rules that make it work. Several carry a **substantiation gate** — a condition that must be true before the ad can be made at all.

---

## 43. Problem Mechanism / Unaware Ad

**Section:** E · **Ratio:** 4:5 · **Type:** Static, Image & Video

An ad that leads with the problem mechanism, not the product. It is built for an unaware audience: people who have the symptoms but don't know the cause. The job is to make them aware of the problem, show them it's bigger than they think, and make it something they won't ignore.

**Creative strategy (static).** Show the problem mechanism physically or as a metaphor. The image should make the viewer feel the problem before they read a word. Gross, uncomfortable, or confronting is fine. No product in this frame.

- Image direction examples: saran wrap over skin for pore-blocking, a wilting plant for dehydration, a cracked surface for dryness.

**Headline starters.** The families that recur:
- Hidden cause: "The real reason [symptom] keeps happening has nothing to do with [common belief]."
- Mechanism reframe: "Most [people like you] don't have a [symptom] problem. They have a [mechanism] problem."
- Hidden enemy / insider: "What [trusted institution] never tells you about [everyday thing]."
- Delayed consequence: "What [common habit] is doing to your [system] right now."
- Unanswerable test: "Can you [simple test]? Most [group] can't."
- Story open on a social reversal, product nowhere in sight.

**Video skeleton: The Missing Input** (10 beats)

1. **Hook.** A fun fact or compliment about the hero (usually the viewer's body). "Break a bone, it heals."
2. **Twist.** The one thing the hero can't do for itself. This sets up the problem. ("It can't make minerals." "Enamel can't grow back.")
3. **Problem mechanism.** Drill into the problem to show expertise and make the viewer take it seriously.
4. **Objection.** Say what they're thinking, agree you thought it too, then show what changed your mind. ("Surely food covers this?")
5. **Villain.** The big enemy the brand and the viewer are both fighting. ("Food refining since the 1940s.")
6. **Fairness.** Concede the obvious counter-argument to show you aren't blinded by bias.
7. **Reframe.** Position the conceded option out of the way: it's fine, but it doesn't fix this case.
8. **Product.** Introduce the product as the solution to the problem you just made them aware of.
9. **Open loop (optional, often cut).** Tease what else it explains, no CTA.
10. **CTA.** Risk reversal plus future-pacing. Paint the first step of the result ("after one day you'll notice"), not the end of the marathon.

Worked examples of the skeleton: a men's mineral supplement ("your body can't make minerals") and Hismile Tooth Armor ("enamel is the one part that can't grow back").

**Rules**
- No product in the static frame. The problem is the hero.
- The mechanism has to be real. A confronting visual of an invented cause is a false claim.
- One problem per ad. A before/after pair is #46 instead.

## 44. Personal Letter / Confessional Ad

**Section:** F · **Ratio:** 1:1 or 4:5 · **Type:** Static

Long-form first-person body copy structured as direct address from a founder, team, or brand voice to the reader. The headline is the emotional opener — "I Owe You An Apology," "We're Saying Goodbye," "We Didn't Expect This." The body reads like a sales letter compressed for feed: paragraphs flow rather than sitting in bands.

Polished but not slick. One hero element (product shot, color block, or branded background) with the body copy as the centerpiece.

**Common emotional registers:** apology, confession, gratitude, scarcity, objection-handling.

**Why it works:** it reads like a message, not an ad. Best for warm and retargeting audiences, high-resistance markets, and sale or inventory moments.

**Distinct from**
- **#43 Problem Mechanism / Unaware** — that format shows the problem with no product in frame. This one is *designed* around the product: branded fonts, intentional color, hero photo or color block, body copy as direct address.
- **#31 Founder Story** — that requires a candid founder portrait on-image. This keeps the founder voice in the copy; no face required.

## 45. Research Receipt / Journal Screenshot Ad

**Section:** B · **Ratio:** 4:5 · **Type:** Static

A credibility-stack ad that borrows authority from a real, published study. The hero isn't the product — it's a *receipt*: a recreated journal article that reframes the problem, built to read as a genuine screenshot at first glance. Then a single bold line lands the new belief, and a real customer comment proves it.

Three acts: **the study earns the right to say it → the line says it → the customer proves it.** Copy is minimal; the journal and the comment do the persuading.

**Structure**

*Journal block (hero, top)* — a recreated research-article panel styled as a real peer-reviewed paper: journal masthead + publisher logo, "Research article," study title, author line, publication date and citation, and a "Result:" line with the one key stat highlighted. Looks like a screenshot, not a designed graphic.

*Reframe line (in the gap between the cards)* — big serif statement that flips the assumption, in two parts:
- Part 1, bold near-black: "It was never the [obvious villain]."
- Part 2, italic in brand accent: "It was [the real cause the study points to]."
- Optional small seal or medallion bridging the two cards.

*Testimonial block (bottom)* — a recreated social comment from a real customer: avatar, name, comment text, timestamp, reaction count. Native platform styling. Looks organic, not branded.

**Substantiation gate**
- The study must be real and verified. Title, authors, journal, year, **and** the stat must actually appear in the paper. The format dies if the receipt is fake — and faking a journal page is fabricating research, not a creative choice.
- Represent the finding honestly. Know the sample size and population before publishing the stat. Don't stretch a narrow result into a universal claim.

**Rules**
- One highlight only. Highlight the single stat line in the Result. More kills the effect.
- Screenshot-accurate, not designed. Flat, straight-on, no perspective tricks, no brand gloss on the journal.
- Order is fixed: authority → reframe → proof. Never lead with the testimonial or the product.
- No product hero. The product barely appears, if at all.
- Works best when a real published study reframes the problem away from the obvious cause toward something the brand is positioned to solve.

**Classification:** defined by journal-screenshot mimicry the way #13 is defined by social-UI mimicry. Tag **Primary 45**, then add secondaries for what's stacked: **13** (testimonial comment), **26** (counter-intuitive reframe), **3** (embedded stat).

## 46. Mechanism Proof / Before-After Body Render Ad

**Section:** E · **Ratio:** 1:1 · **Type:** Static

Product paired with a before/after visualization of its mechanism inside the body or material — a rendered "here's what's happening, before vs. after." The visual *is* the proof: it shows the physical or physiological change the product drives. Fuses the transformation logic of #11 with the authority render of #30.

The power of the format and its danger are the same thing: a before/after body render reads as a clinical claim whether or not you say it out loud.

**Structure**
- *Product (anchor)* — clean product shot on one side of the frame.
- *Before render* — labeled "Today / Before." Shows the problem state physically: buildup, blockage, dryness, inflammation. The actual mechanism, not a metaphor.
- *After render* — labeled "Later / After." Shows the improved state the product *verifiably* produces. Connecting arrow optional.
- *Headline* — names the mechanism plainly: what is changing and why.

**Substantiation gate (non-negotiable)**
- The before/after may only depict an outcome the product can substantiate it causes. If it can't, do not make this ad — choose another format. A "concept illustration" or "not medical advice" disclaimer does **not** rescue an unsubstantiable render; the image is the claim.
- **No disease claims.** No non-drug product (supplement, cosmetic, uncleared device) showing that it treats, shrinks, reverses, or cures a diagnosed condition. That's an unapproved-drug claim regardless of framing, and it's heavily enforced.
- Represent magnitude and speed honestly. No exaggerating the size or rate of change beyond what is demonstrated.
- Mechanism, not metaphor. If you show anatomy, it must be accurate to what actually happens.

**Best for** products with a real, physical, demonstrable mechanism — filtration, descaling, barrier repair, mechanical action, a substantiated cosmetic mechanism.

**Classification:** tag **Primary 46**, secondaries **11** (before/after structure) and **30** (mechanism render). A problem visual with no product and no after state is **#43** instead.

## 47. AI Animation Style Ads

**Section:** E · **Ratio:** 1:1 & 9:16 · **Type:** Image & Video

AI-generated animation in a deliberately non-default aesthetic. The default AI animation look — Pixar-style 3D and claymation — is saturated. This format is about picking a material style that reads as intentional craft instead.

**Style options**

Felt stop-motion · plush / toy animation · puppet or marionette · animatronic · plastic doll · action figure · vinyl toy · wooden doll · paper cut-out · cardboard craft · brick-built · miniature model · diorama · wax figure · porcelain doll · balloon / inflatable character · gel / translucent plastic · metallic robot · glass / crystal character.

**Rules**
- Pick one material and hold it for the whole spot. Mixed materials read as an AI artifact rather than a style.
- The material should have a reason — a plush execution for a kids' product, brick-built for something modular. Arbitrary style choices look like novelty for its own sake.
- Physical-material styles tolerate AI imperfection better than photoreal humans do, which is much of why they work.

## 48. Problem Deep Dive Yapper Ad

**Section:** E · **Ratio:** 9:16 · **Type:** Video Script

A faceless, prop-driven demo video where a single voice teaches the *root cause* of a problem before ever mentioning a product. No face on camera — just hands, physical props, and a calm expert-next-door voiceover.

The creator spends roughly **80% of the runtime on genuine education** (why the problem happens, demonstrated with real objects) and only ~20% pitching. Because the viewer feels taught rather than sold, the product lands as the natural conclusion of the lesson.

Cheap to produce, endlessly repeatable, portable across any audience with a teachable problem.

**Formula:** hyper-specific audience/pain hook → teach the root-cause mechanism with props → agitate and compound the stakes → earned pivot ("so what's the fix?") + kill the old way → product reveal with feature→benefit mapped back to the lesson → offer-stack CTA.

**Production note**

9:16 vertical, ~2:00–2:30. Faceless — only hands, props, and the product appear. Handheld, close-up or top-down, casual rather than studio-polished. One continuous conversational voiceover that sounds unscripted. Hold the value-to-pitch ratio near 80/20.

*Props to gather:* an authority prop (textbook, diagram, spec sheet), a demo model that shows the mechanism, a pointer (a pen works), the "wrong way" object, and the hero product.

**Beats**

**[Hook — hyper-specific audience + pain]**
Talk track: "If you're a [SPECIFIC_AUDIENCE] and you keep [SYMPTOM_1], [SYMPTOM_2], even [SYMPTOM_3] — understand it all stems from [ROOT_CAUSE]."
B-roll: hands holding or pointing at the [WRONG_WAY_OBJECT]; keyword caption naming the audience.

**[Teach — root-cause mechanism]**
Talk track: "In this [SITUATION] you're putting pressure on [MECHANISM]… which means [CONSEQUENCE]… and that's what's actually causing [SYMPTOM]."
B-roll: point at the [AUTHORITY_PROP] and [DEMO_MODEL] with the pointer; physically show what goes wrong. This is the bulk of the video — take your time.

**[Authority drop — woven in, not front-loaded]**
Talk track: "When I worked as a [CREDENTIAL], I recommended this all the time…"
B-roll: keep demonstrating. The credential is dropped casually mid-lesson, never as an opening flex.

**[Agitate — compound the stakes]**
Talk track: "And when [PROBLEM] happens, [SECONDARY_PROBLEM] happens too — you're making it worse when you think you're making it better."
B-roll: demonstrate the knock-on effect with the props.

**[Pivot — earned, and kill the old way]**
Talk track: "So what's the fix? First, get rid of [WRONG_WAY_OBJECT] — here's exactly why it fails you…"
B-roll: demonstrate the flaw of the old way.

**[Reveal — product + feature→benefit]**
Talk track: "That's why I recommend [PRODUCT]. It has [FEATURE_1] for [PAIN_1], and [FEATURE_2] for [PAIN_2]…"
B-roll: reveal the product; point to each feature and tie it back to the mechanism you taught.

**[CTA — offer stack]**
Talk track: social proof → risk reversal → price anchor → urgency → one direct CTA.
B-roll: final beauty shot; caption with the offer.

**Remix logic**

1. **New niche, same skeleton** — swap audience, symptoms, root cause, and prop set. Keep beat order and the 80/20 ratio identical.
2. **Same niche, new product/angle** — keep the audience and the taught mechanism, swap the pivot and hero product.

Don't rewrite a winning script from scratch. Isolate one variable. Load-bearing parts: faceless execution, the 80/20 value ratio, root-cause teaching by physical demonstration, the woven-in authority drop, the earned pivot, feature→benefit mapped to the lesson, and the full offer-stack close.

**Length:** no fixed target. Runtime is driven by how much teaching the mechanism requires. Write the full value-first script, then trim for placement — but never so far that the 80/20 ratio breaks.

## 49. Destroy The Common Belief Explainer

**Section:** E · **Ratio:** 9:16 · **Type:** Video Script

A faceless or low-production explainer that sells by **changing what the viewer believes before it ever mentions the product.** It opens by naming a belief the customer already holds and destroying it, then walks them through the *real* mechanism behind their problem. The product appears near the end as the inevitable conclusion.

Shot one of two ways: a **two-voice myth-bust** (Voice A states the common belief, the expert corrects it) or a **single expert voiceover** over hands, props, or a talking head. No studio polish — it should feel like a knowledgeable friend setting you straight.

**When to use it:** when your product wins because the customer has to understand *why* it's different before they'll buy. Supplements, skincare, health, and any category crowded with cheaper look-alikes and a lot of misinformation. If a false belief is what's blocking the sale, destroy the belief first and sell second.

**The 13 beats**

1. **Destroy the old belief** *(hook)* — "Let's bust one of the biggest myths about [PROBLEM]: '[COMMON_BELIEF].' That's wrong." Must land in the first ~3 seconds.
2. **Reveal the hidden enemy** — "What you think is [HARMLESS_THING] is actually [REAL_ENEMY]."
3. **Teach the real mechanism** — "Here's what's actually happening: [MECHANISM, in plain language]." Demonstrate with a prop, diagram, or close-up. This earns your authority.
4. **Raise the stakes** — "If you don't deal with it, [ESCALATION — spreads, worsens, gets more expensive]."
5. **Explain why current solutions fail** — "[COMMON_FIX] only [SURFACE_ACTION], it never touches [ROOT_CAUSE]."
6. **Create curiosity for the real solution** — tease the mechanism without naming the product.
7. **Introduce the new mechanism** *(product reveal)* — "That's why I started using [PRODUCT]. It works by [UNIQUE_MECHANISM]."
8. **Prove why the mechanism works** — show the ingredient, the spec, the demo.
9. **Handle objections naturally** — pre-empt the #1 objection ("it's not that serious," "too good to be true," "I've tried everything") in one line.
10. **Stack credibility and proof** — credential, review count, before/afters, adoption numbers.
11. **Sell the future identity** — "Imagine [THE AFTER STATE — who they become once the problem is gone]."
12. **Reduce buying risk** — money-back guarantee, free returns, risk-free trial.
13. **Call to action** — one action plus a reason to act now.

Each beat removes one specific reason not to buy. Don't skip beats.

**Production note:** 9:16 vertical, ~30–90s. Low-fi and authentic. Conversational, sounds unscripted. Front-load the belief destruction — that *is* the hook. The product does not appear until beat 7.

**Remix logic**

1. **New niche, same skeleton** — swap belief, enemy, mechanism, product. Keep the 13-beat order and the teach-before-pitch ratio.
2. **Same niche, new belief** — keep audience and product, attack a *different* false belief. One product usually supports several, each opening on a different myth.

Load-bearing: belief destruction in the first ~3 seconds, teaching the mechanism *before* the product appears, the product entering only as the "new mechanism," and the full close (proof → identity → risk reversal → CTA).

**As a static**
- **Single image** = the myth-bust headline. Put the false belief on screen with a hard correction. That's **#26**, with the destroyed belief as the headline.
- **Carousel** = the full sequence, one beat per card: destroy the belief (**#37** hook slide) → hidden enemy → mechanism → stakes → why fixes fail → product-as-answer → CTA.
- **Atomize a beat** — the mechanism stands alone as **#30**; "current fixes fail" maps to **#7** or **#8**.

A static carries *one* load-bearing beat. Lead with the destroyed belief; let the carousel or landing page teach the mechanism.

## 50. Long-Form Full-Funnel VSL (Common Enemy)

**Section:** F · **Ratio:** 9:16 · **Type:** Video Script

A long-form (~2:30–3:30) UGC selfie testimonial that behaves like a video sales letter compressed into one first-person story. A single avatar who looks like the target customer walks a *cold* viewer through the entire awareness ladder in one sitting — it doesn't assume awareness, it **creates** it.

**The engine is a stacked common enemy.** Two villains doing two different jobs:

1. **An institution the prospect already distrusts** — the dismissive expert, the greedy industry. Creates *emotional alliance*.
2. **The cheap or fake version of the solution** — the grocery-store version, the marketplace knockoff. Creates *logical exoneration*: "it's not that the category doesn't work, and it's not that you failed — they sold you the wrong version." This neutralizes the #1 objection ("I already tried that") before it can form.

Every hard claim is attributed to a **third-party authority** — a named study, a foundation, an expert — never the brand. The testimonial only reports her own experience, which keeps the promises credible.

**When to use it:** cold or problem-aware traffic, higher-consideration or higher-price products, and any category where the prospect has already tried the cheap version and been burned. This is the heavy-artillery cousin of the short yappers (#48, #42, #14) — reach for those for warm traffic, this when you have to build belief from zero.

**The 12 beats**

1. **Demonstration hook + open loop** — "See this? This was me [TIMEFRAME] ago — [CONCRETE BEFORE-STATE DEMO]. Now look. And it's all because I figured out why [COMMON_SOLUTION] wasn't working for me." Literal before/after in the first ~10s. The loop promises a *reason*, not a product.
2. **Agitate the problem** — life consequences, not symptoms. Job, relationship, independence, identity. Concrete scenes.
3. **Enemy #1: the institution** — "[AUTHORITY_FIGURE] told me [SCARY OR DISMISSIVE RECOMMENDATION]." Make fear of the conventional fix exceed fear of the problem. A smug prediction here plants a revenge arc.
4. **Failed-solutions tour (exonerate)** — "I tried [V1], [V2], [V3] — did everything the internet said. For months, nothing." Message: it's not your fault.
5. **Epiphany / discovery** — "Then I found [THIRD-PARTY STUDY / FOUNDATION / EXPERT], and it explained everything." Borrowed authority; the source makes the claims.
6. **Unique mechanism (Enemy #2: the fake solution)** — "Most [CATEGORY] only has [FLAW]. Your body can't even use it. What you actually need is [CRITERION 1], [2], [3]." The heart of the ad. A vivid waste image makes it stick.
7. **Product reveal as mechanism-match** — "It has everything the research said I needed: [1 ✓], [2 ✓], [3 ✓]." The study already sold it; the product just fulfills the criteria.
8. **Results timeline** — "First week? Nothing crazy. By week two, [SMALL MILESTONE]. By week three, [BIGGER]. Within a month, [FULL PAYOFF]." **Admitting week one did nothing is the single most trust-building line in the format.**
9. **Future-pace the transformation** — a specific after-state scene that mirrors the pain from beat 2.
10. **Damaging admission + scarcity** — "[BRAND] is a small company and they sell out constantly — because they won't cut corners on [QUALITY]." Reframe the flaw as proof of quality. Reason-why scarcity, not a fake countdown.
11. **Qualified CTA** — "If you're [SEGMENT 1], [SEGMENT 2], or [SEGMENT 3] — click the link." Name every segment out loud so each viewer hears themselves.
12. **Callback close** — close the revenge arc; callback to the villain or the open loop. Warm peer-to-peer sign-off.

**Production note:** one avatar cast to *look like the customer*, talking to camera UGC-selfie style. Handheld, unpolished, one continuous first-person take. It's her story, not a brand ad. **The product does not appear until roughly the 60–70% mark.** Stack both enemies. Launder every claim through a named third-party authority.

**Remix logic**

1. **New niche, same skeleton** — swap pain, both enemies, mechanism, product. Keep the awareness-ladder order and the two-enemy stack.
2. **Same niche, new lead enemy** — swap which villain leads (institution-first vs. fake-solution-first) to test a different emotional entry point.

Load-bearing: value-first pacing, the two-enemy stack, claim-laundering through a third party, the honest "nothing happened week one," the mechanism-as-checklist reveal, and the callback bookend.

**As a static**
- **Carousel** is the closest analog — same ladder slide by slide: hook/before-after (**#37**) → problem → the enemy → the mechanism → product-as-match → proof → CTA.
- **Atomize the strongest beat** — the mechanism comparison becomes **#8** or **#7**; the transformation becomes **#11**; the borrowed-authority study becomes **#45**; a testimonial line becomes **#9**.

Pick the mechanism or the before/after — those do the most persuasive work.

## 51. Native Ad (Organic-Mimic)

**Section:** G · **Ratio:** per variant · **Type:** Static, Image & Video

An ad built to pass as an organic post — something a real person would actually share with their own followers. Not "authentic-looking branded content." An actual post.

It has two jobs and has to do both:

**1. Disguise.** Nothing on the creative signals "ad." No logo lockup, no brand font, no design system, no CTA baked into the image, no copy block over a stock photo. If a designer touched it and you can tell, it's dead.

**2. Curiosity.** Because the creative doesn't explain itself, the only way to find out what it's about is to tap *See more*. The click is the mechanic — the ad withholds context on purpose.

The second job is the one that gets skipped. A raw photo with no hook is just a bad ad with no design. The image has to **provoke a question the viewer needs answered** — a fascination written as a picture instead of a sentence.

**Variants**

**51a — Native Reel (4:3 in 9:16).** A 4:3 video inside a 9:16 frame, black bars top and bottom, first-person caption set in the black space above the video. Mimics the trending-reel format people post natively. Caption is confessional and mid-story. Spec: 1080×1920 frame, 4:3 video centered, caption in the upper bar, system-default sans — never a brand font.

**51b — Native Static, No Copy.** A single raw photo, zero overlay. All the work is done by the visual; the caption below the image carries the pitch. Spec: 4:5 or 1:1, phone-camera quality, imprecise crop, no color grade.

**51c — Native Long-Caption Post.** Plain or incidental image plus a long first-person caption written so it truncates at the "…" mid-thought. The truncation *is* the CTA. Best written as a run-on qualifying opener — name the reader's exact situation in enough detail that they recognize themselves, then cut off right before the payoff.

**51d — Native Editorial / Advertorial.** Reads as a real article, listicle, or news item. Publisher-style layout, not brand-style. Offers the service indirectly — the article earns the trust, the offer arrives after.

**Visual curiosity devices**

Use these the way you'd use a rhetorical device in a headline. Pick one — stacking two reads as staged.

- **Juxtaposition** — two things that don't belong in the same frame
- **Unexplained action** — someone mid-activity with no visible reason for it
- **Mid-emotion capture** — crying, laughing, stunned, mid-sentence. Emotion without a cause demands the cause
- **Withheld object** — the subject reacting to something off-frame or out of focus
- **Wrong context** — a familiar object somewhere it has no business being
- **Aftermath** — the result of an event rather than the event, so the viewer reconstructs what happened
- **Direct camera address** — a real, un-retouched face looking straight down the lens. No caption, no smile, no product

**Rules**
- No brand marks anywhere in the frame. Whitelist through a personal or creator profile where possible.
- Phone-camera quality is correct. Slight blur, bad lighting, awkward crop — all fine, all helping.
- No overlay text on 51b. If the image needs a caption to make sense, the image failed.
- Captions are first-person and mid-story. Never brand voice. Never "Introducing."
- **The image must not answer the question it raises.** If a viewer can tell what's being sold from the thumbnail, there's no reason to click.
- Expect high CTR and softer downstream conversion. This format buys clicks with curiosity, so the landing page has to pay the question off in the first screen. **Judge it on CPL/CPA, never CTR alone.**
- Disclosure still applies. Native styling is an aesthetic choice, not a way to hide that it's paid — the *Sponsored* label stays, and the claim still has to be true.

**Ratios:** 51a → 9:16 · 51b → 4:5 or 1:1 · 51c → 1:1 or 4:5 · 51d → 4:5.

**Distinct from**
- **#13 Social Media Comment Screenshot** — a screenshot *of* social proof, used as evidence. This is a post that *is* the content.
- **#14 UGC-Style / Selfie Review** — UGC still performs a review to camera. Native makes no claim and often names no product.
- **#38 Meme-Style** — also native to platform culture, but comedic and shareable-first. Native is sincere and curiosity-first.
- **#43 Problem Mechanism / Unaware** — still visibly an ad: a headline over a problem visual. Native carries no ad signals at all.
- **#44 Personal Letter / Confessional** — same first-person voice, but *designed*. Native is undesigned by rule.
- **#36 Instagram Story / 9:16** — a placement spec, not a styling approach. 51a can run in that placement.

## 52. Science / Mechanism Explainer Ad

**Section:** E · **Ratio:** 1:1 & 9:16 · **Type:** Static

Most ads make a claim and back it with a proof point — a stat, a checkmark, a testimonial. A mechanism explainer does something different: it teaches the audience the underlying *process* behind a claim, so they arrive at the conclusion themselves instead of being told it.

This matters most when a product's core claim is unusual enough that people have no mental model for it. An unexplained claim about something unfamiliar reads as marketing spin. An *explained* one reads as engineering. The reaction you're going for is "Oh, I get it now. That actually makes sense."

**The psychological lever:** causal understanding builds more trust than repetition. Saying "30% faster" five times is weaker than showing someone, once, *why* it's true.

**Not right for every product.** It works when there's a genuine, explainable mechanism behind the claim, and when the audience's default assumption about the problem is wrong or incomplete. If the claim is just "we're better," there's no mechanism to teach.

**The four beats**

1. **Problem — name the assumption you're about to overturn.** Start with what the audience already believes is happening. Not the product's problem — the *reader's* current, incomplete explanation for it. Skip this and the ad has nothing to overturn.
2. **Mechanism — explain the actual "how," in plain language.** The core of the ad and the part normal ad copy skips. Walk through the real causal process: not a claim, but "here's specifically what's physically happening, step by step." Good mechanism copy reads like an explanation to a smart, curious person who's never heard it before.
3. **Proof — why the mechanism is credible, not invented.** Tie it to something checkable: a documented phenomenon, a named process, a real number, a credible source category. The proof sits at the *mechanism* level, not the outcome level.
4. **Outcome — translate the mechanism into the benefit.** The only place a benefit or claim belongs, and it feels earned because you just explained why it's true. Category claims (warranties, speed, pricing) belong here — making them *before* explaining the mechanism is what makes ads feel like marketing instead of insight.

**Don't stack mechanisms.** The format works because it's singularly focused on one "aha." Combining it with unrelated offers collapses it into a feature-list ad.

**Worked example — rubber paving vs. concrete (freeze-thaw)**

1. **Problem:** Most homeowners assume concrete cracks from ordinary wear and tear — foot traffic, age, "that's just what happens."
2. **Mechanism:** Concrete is porous. Water gets into those pores. When that water freezes it expands, and the pressure that expansion creates inside the material is dramatically higher than the stress concrete can withstand before cracking. Every winter freeze is a small structural event happening invisibly inside the slab, compounding year over year — which is why the same spot keeps failing. It isn't new damage each time; it's the same mechanism repeating.
3. **Proof:** This is a documented failure mode in concrete engineering (freeze-thaw damage), not a claim the brand invented. The counter-mechanism — a flexible rubber surface with a polyurethane binder — is presented as the logical answer to the mechanism just explained: if rigid material loses to expansion pressure, a surface that flexes with that pressure instead of resisting it is the direct fix.
4. **Outcome:** Because the fix addresses the actual mechanism rather than just being "a better product," the brand can confidently double the industry-standard warranty.

Note the shape: three beats of physics, one beat of claim. The warranty lands as a conclusion, not a boast.

## 53. Trigger Event / Drama Story Ad

**Section:** F · **Ratio:** 1:1 & 9:16 (9:16 for animation, song, and yapper cuts; 1:1 or 4:5 for feed and native statics) · **Type:** AI Animation, Video Script, Image & Video

A story ad built around one trigger event: the exact moment a customer's problem stopped being tolerable. "I have trouble sleeping" is a problem. "My mother-in-law told me I looked exhausted in front of my husband's entire family at Thanksgiving" is a trigger event.

The ad cold-opens on that scene, walks through the spiral that followed, shows how they found the product and the one reason it worked, and ends back in the same scene with the problem solved. The product shows up late, sometimes around the 2-minute mark. It is a story structure first and a production format second. Source: Alex Cooper (October 2026), who calls it a trigger event ad; on X these are called AI drama ads.

**Where it's winning (October 2026):** Quasi (skincare) runs 125 AI animation variations off seven trigger types, and its top three ads by impressions are all trigger event animations (the #1 runs 10 minutes). It has also worked for supplements and a toothbrush. Longer cuts win more, especially with older audiences.

**Why it works**
1. **Recognition does the targeting.** People who lived the scene stop scrolling. Everyone else keeps going.
2. **Story earns belief before any claim.** By Act 4 the viewer wants the explanation.
3. **One winner becomes dozens of ads.** Rotate the trigger, the character, or the format.

**Reach for it when** the problem has a social or emotional cost, every competitor makes the same claims, you have reviews or comments to mine, and cold traffic is the target. **Skip it** for impulse buys and routine reorders, or when the only trigger you can find is generic ("I wanted to save money").

**The five acts** (product never enters before Act 3)
1. **Trigger event.** Cold open on the moment. Give it a place, a witness, and a detail.
2. **Rock bottom.** More moments that make it worse, plus the failed fixes. The failed fixes tell the viewer it wasn't their fault.
3. **Discovery.** The product is found inside the story: a friend's tip, a shelf, a drawer in a salon. Never a pitch, never a logo hero shot.
4. **Mechanism.** One named reason it works when everything else didn't. Show it on screen.
5. **Payoff.** Back to the Act 1 place with the same witness, transformed. Then a guarantee and the CTA.

Holding rules: the Act 1 scene is both hook and payoff; one trigger event per ad; one mechanism in Act 4.

**A strong trigger event has four parts:** place (Thanksgiving dinner, Target, a bathroom stall at a wedding), witness (the mother-in-law, the ex), detail (the word that stung, the number of people at the table), and turning point (the moment they decided they were done).

| Weak (a condition) | Strong (a moment) |
|---|---|
| "I was tired all the time." | "My mother-in-law told me I looked exhausted in front of 14 people." |
| "I was self-conscious about my teeth." | "Nobody told me to stop smiling in photos. I just did." |

**Quasi's seven trigger types:** the run-in, the discovery of a betrayal, the overheard word, the milestone countdown, the intimacy retreat, the handover ritual, the exhaustion point. Keep the type fixed and rotate the place, witness, and character.

**Finding trigger events.** Customers already describe them; never invent one. Look in long emotional reviews, ad comments, post-purchase surveys ("What made you finally decide to fix this?"), category Reddit threads, and for service clients, sales call notes and intake forms. Mining prompt for pasted reviews, comments, and survey answers:

```txt
You're mining voice-of-customer data for trigger events.

A trigger event is the specific moment a customer's problem stopped being tolerable and they decided to fix it. It's a scene you could film: a place, a witness, and a line someone said or a detail they noticed.

From the reviews, comments, and survey answers below:
1. Pull every passage that describes a specific moment rather than a general complaint.
2. Quote the customer's exact words.
3. Rewrite each one as a one-line, first-person cold open.
4. Name the place, the witness, and the detail. Mark any that are missing.
5. Rate each one 1 to 5 on specificity. Drop anything under 3.
6. Group them by trigger type (run-in, overheard comment, milestone, exhaustion point, discovery, other).

Do not invent or embellish anything the customer didn't say.

[PASTE VOC HERE]
```

**Production formats** (the storyline is the asset)

| Format | How | Related |
|---|---|---|
| AI animation | Script in Claude, scenes in Higgsfield (Kling 3.0 or Seedance) | #47 |
| Song ad | Five acts as lyrics, track in Suno, scenes in Higgsfield | #47 |
| Yapper | A creator who looks like the customer tells it to camera | #42, #50 |
| Native ad | Act 1 is the first line of primary text, cut before the payoff, with a raw image | #51c |
| Slideshow | One act per frame, Act 1 as the hook frame | #41 |

Ship whichever format is fastest to produce for the client, then port the winner. To scale, change one variable at a time: trigger place, character (age or situation), or format.

**Substantiation gate**
- Trigger events come from real customer words. If a review describes it, you can dramatize it.
- Act 4's mechanism is a claim inside a story and needs the same proof as #30, #45, #46, and #52.
- A dramatized narrator must not be presented as a real, named customer. Act 5 results must match what a typical customer gets.
- Keep the brand out of Acts 1 and 2.
- Narrate in first person. Meta's personal attributes policy blocks copy that asserts things about the viewer ("Embarrassed by your acne?"). "I started angling my face in photos" is the safe version.
- Judge on CPA. On long cuts, check where viewers drop off. If they leave before Act 3, the trigger event isn't landing.

**Distinct from**
- **#5 PAS** — opens on a problem category and gets to the product fast. 53 opens on one filmed moment and holds the product until Act 3.
- **#34 Emotional Trigger / FOMO** — uses "trigger" for a with/without static split. Here it means the moment the problem became intolerable.
- **#44 Personal Letter** — a designed static in the brand's voice. 53 is a customer's story told in scenes.
- **#47 AI Animation Style** — a visual style. They stack: a trigger event script in felt stop-motion is Primary 53, Secondary 47.
- **#50 Long-Form VSL** — runs on two enemies and a third-party authority, always a selfie testimonial. 53 runs on one bookended scene in any format.
- **#42 Outcome-First Yapper** — opens on the result. 53 opens on the worst moment.
